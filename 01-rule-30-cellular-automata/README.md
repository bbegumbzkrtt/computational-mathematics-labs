# Rule 30: Elementary Cellular Automaton

This directory contains a Python implementation and visualization of **Rule 30**, one of the 256 elementary cellular automata introduced by Stephen Wolfram in 1983.

---

## What is Rule 30?

Rule 30 is a one-dimensional cellular automaton governed by a set of simple, local transition rules. Despite its structural simplicity, it is globally famous for generating **deterministic chaos**—complex, non-repeating patterns emerging from a completely ordered initial state.

### How the State Transitions Work

The simulation consists of a row of binary cells (0 or 1). At each discrete time step, a cell's new state is evaluated based on its own current state and the states of its left and right immediate neighbors.

The 8 possible neighborhood configurations and their resulting outcomes for Rule 30 are defined as follows:

| Current Neighborhood (L, C, R) | 111 | 110 | 101 | 100 | 011 | 010 | 001 | 000 |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| **New Cell State** | **0** | **0** | **0** | **1** | **1** | **1** | **1** | **0** |

---

## Key Characteristics & Significance

- **Order vs. Chaos:** Starting from a single active cell, the left boundary expands in a perfectly structured diagonal pattern, whereas the central and right sections evolve into pseudo-random, chaotic structures.
- **Pseudo-Random Number Generation:** The sequence of states produced in the central vertical column exhibits such strong randomness properties that it has historically been used as a random number generator in computational systems.
- **Natural Occurrence:** The intricate, triangular patterns generated by Rule 30 closely mirror natural patterns found in biology, such as the shell design of the *Conus textile* cone snail.
